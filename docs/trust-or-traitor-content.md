# Trust or Traitor: content for teacher review

**Status: DRAFT for the teacher to check. Nothing here is in the game yet.**

This is every fact the new feature will show students:
- **Part 1: the Archive**, a set of declassified files, one per fact, in six drawers.
- **Part 2: the intel reports** that appear on cards. Each report has a true version, the fake a double agent can swap in, and a third choice for the "prove it" step.

**How to read it:**
- Text in `[[double brackets]]` is **redacted** in the game: a black bar students hover over to reveal. Redactions cover the decisive details (numbers, dates, places) and real-history outcomes that could spoil a round.
- Revealing a redaction *during an interrogation* marks that verdict "with research" (2 points instead of 3).
- Every fact comes from the game's fact-checked history (`docs/fact-check.md`) or the facts checked for the mini lesson. Nothing is new invention.
- The files carry no invented file numbers, initials or dates. Their headers only name the service.

## Part 1: The Archive

### Drawer 1: HEADLINES (shared events every student plays)

**File: The Pyry meeting · 1939** (Polish Cipher Bureau)
In [[July 1939]], Polish cryptologists met British and French intelligence officers in the Pyry forest near [[Warsaw]]. The Poles had been reading Germany's Enigma machine since [[1932]], and they showed their reconstructed machines and the methods they had used. Britain and France were each promised a replica Enigma. About five weeks later, Germany invaded Poland.

**File: The capture of U-110 · 1941** (Admiralty)
On [[9 May 1941]], ships of the Royal Navy forced the German submarine U-110 to the surface south of [[Iceland]]. A boarding party from HMS Bulldog took its [[Enigma machine and codebooks]] before the U-boat sank while being towed. Britain kept the capture secret so Germany would believe the boat had simply been lost. The British sailors involved were sworn to silence.

**File: The carriers go silent · 1941** (US Navy)
In late November 1941, Japan's carrier force sailed toward Hawaii under strict [[radio silence]]. US Navy listening posts had tracked those ships by their radio traffic, and now they lost them. The attack on Pearl Harbor came on [[7 December 1941]]. The planes launched from about [[230 miles north]] of Oahu.

**File: Water at Midway · 1942** (US Navy)
US Navy codebreakers in Hawaii could read parts of Japan's main naval code, [[JN-25]]. Japanese messages named a target called "AF". To prove it was [[Midway]], Midway sent an uncoded message saying its water plant had broken, and Japanese messages soon reported that AF was [[short of fresh water]]. Admiral Nimitz placed his carriers to ambush the Japanese fleet, and in early June 1942 US planes sank [[four]] Japanese carriers.

**File: Operation Vengeance · 1943** (US Navy)
Codebreakers read a radio message giving the times and places of Admiral [[Yamamoto]]'s inspection flight in the Solomon Islands. On [[18 April 1943]], US Army [[P-38]] fighters flying from Guadalcanal shot down his plane over [[Bougainville]]. To protect the codebreakers, the Americans let it be believed that [[coastwatchers]] had spotted his flight.

**File: Operation Fortitude · 1944** (Allied deception staff)
Before D-Day, the Allies invented a whole army in southeast England, commanded on paper by General [[Patton]]. Fake radio traffic, dummy equipment and double agents made Germany expect the main landing at [[Calais]]. Even after the Normandy landings on [[6 June 1944]], Germany held back its 15th Army near Calais [[for weeks]].

### Drawer 2: BLETCHLEY PARK

**File: The first bombe · 1940** (Government Code and Cypher School)
At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman designed an electro-mechanical machine called the [[bombe]] to test thousands of possible Enigma settings at speed. The first one began work in [[1940]]. German army and air force settings changed every day at [[midnight]], so every morning the search started again.

**File: Shark · 1942** (Government Code and Cypher School, Hut 8)
On [[1 February 1942]], German U-boats switched to a [[four-rotor]] Enigma that Bletchley called Shark. For about [[ten months]], Bletchley could not read U-boat orders. Codebooks seized from the sinking U-boat [[U-559]] in October 1942 helped break Shark that December.

**File: The women of Bletchley · 1939–1945** (Government Code and Cypher School)
By 1945, nearly [[10,000]] people worked at Bletchley Park and its outstations. About [[three-quarters]] of them were women, who ran the bombes, handled and translated messages, and broke codes themselves. Mavis Lever (later Batey) helped break an Italian naval cipher before the Battle of Cape [[Matapan]] in 1941.

**File: The Mincemeat decrypts · 1943** (Government Code and Cypher School, Hut 3)
After the body carrying fake invasion plans was found off Spain, Bletchley's decrypts were the only way to know whether Germany had believed it. The decrypts showed German forces moving toward [[Greece and Sardinia]]. Prime Minister Churchill was told: "Mincemeat [[swallowed rod, line and sinker]]."

**File: Colossus · 1944** (Government Code and Cypher School)
Engineer [[Tommy Flowers]] and his team built Colossus, often called the world's first programmable electronic computer. It helped break [[Tunny]], the cipher used by Hitler's high command. By 1945, [[ten]] Colossus machines were working. Messages read just before D-Day showed Germany still expected the main attack at [[Calais]].

### Drawer 3: NAVY

**File: JN-25 · 1939** (US Navy, Washington)
In [[June 1939]], the Japanese navy began using a new code, later called JN-25. Its messages were turned into [[five-digit]] number groups, then hidden again under a second layer of numbers. A small team of US Navy codebreakers worked on it with card-sorting machines, and by Pearl Harbor it was only [[partly]] readable.

**File: The Sinkov mission · 1941** (US Army and Navy)
In January 1941, [[four]] American codebreakers sailed for Britain aboard the battleship HMS King George V. They reached Bletchley Park in [[February 1941]], bringing Purple machines, built to read Japan's diplomatic cipher, as a gift. In return they were shown Bletchley's work, the start of a codebreaking partnership that lasted the whole war.

**File: "We don't know where they are" · 1941** (US Pacific Fleet)
In early December 1941, the Pacific Fleet's intelligence officer, [[Edwin Layton]], told Admiral Kimmel that he did not know where Japan's carriers were. Saying so was uncomfortable, but it was honest. Days later, the carriers attacked [[Pearl Harbor]].

**File: Station HYPO · 1942** (US Navy, Hawaii)
Station HYPO, the Navy's codebreaking unit in Hawaii, was led by [[Joseph Rochefort]]. It worked in a windowless basement at Pearl Harbor. Using HYPO's work, Layton predicted the Japanese carriers would appear about [[175 miles]] northwest of Midway early on [[4 June 1942]]. The prediction was close to where they were found.

**File: Agnes Meyer Driscoll** (US Navy, Washington)
Agnes Meyer Driscoll was one of the US Navy's leading codebreakers between the wars. She broke [[earlier Japanese naval codes]] in the 1920s and 1930s, and she trained many of the codebreakers who later worked on JN-25.

### Drawer 4: RESISTANCE

**File: The machine reaches London · 1939** (French intelligence)
In [[August 1939]], French intelligence officer [[Gustave Bertrand]] carried one of the Polish replica Enigma machines to London in the diplomatic bag. He handed it to Stewart Menzies of British intelligence, who later became its head, at [[Victoria Station]].

**File: Watching the U-boat bases · 1941** (Resistance networks)
French resistance networks watched the U-boat bases at Brest, Lorient and [[Saint-Nazaire]] and reported sailings to London, often at great risk. Their reports also helped hide a bigger secret: Britain could already read many U-boat orders.

**File: The Channel Dash · 1942** (Resistance networks)
On the night of [[11 February 1942]], the German battleships [[Scharnhorst and Gneisenau]] and the cruiser Prinz Eugen slipped out of Brest. London knew they would soon sail, but they were not spotted until they were almost at [[Dover]], too late to stop them. All three reached German waters.

**File: The rocket secret · 1943–1944** (Polish Home Army)
Polish Home Army reports helped Britain find Germany's secret rocket base at [[Peenemünde]], which the RAF bombed in [[August 1943]]. In May 1944 a test V-2 rocket came down by the Bug River, and the Home Army hid it from the Germans. In July 1944, parts of the [[V-2]] were flown out to Britain from a secret landing field, by way of Italy.

**File: Verlaine on the BBC · 1944** (Special Operations Executive)
Just before D-Day, the BBC broadcast lines from Paul Verlaine's poem "Autumn Song" as a signal to resistance networks that the invasion was coming. German intelligence knew what the lines meant, but the warning was [[not acted on in time]]. On the night of D-Day, resistance groups [[cut rail lines and telephone cables]] across France.

### Drawer 5: MI5

**File: Agent SNOW · 1939** (Security Service)
Arthur Owens was a Welsh engineer who had been passing information to German intelligence in [[Hamburg]]. Early in 1939 the Germans sent him a secret radio set, and he handed it to the British authorities. He was detained in [[September 1939]], and MI5 ran him as its [[first double agent]] of the war, code name SNOW. MI5 wrote his messages, feeding true but harmless details so his German handlers kept trusting him.

**File: Double Cross · 1941** (Security Service)
From [[January 1941]], a group called the Twenty Committee, chaired by [[J. C. Masterman]], oversaw Britain's double agents. Agents were fed "[[chicken feed]]": true but minor details, mixed with believable inventions. Decoded German intelligence messages let MI5 check whether the Germans still trusted each agent.

**File: Popov's questions · 1941** (Security Service)
The double agent Dušan Popov arrived in the United States in [[August 1941]] with a list of questions from his German handlers. Many were detailed questions about the defenses at [[Pearl Harbor]]. The questionnaire reached the FBI, but the warning was [[not taken seriously]].

**File: Garbo's network · 1942** (Security Service)
Juan Pujol García, a Spaniard, offered to spy for Britain and was turned down, so he got German intelligence to hire him instead. From Lisbon he invented reports about Britain from a guidebook, maps and newsreels. He reached London in [[April 1942]], where MI5 code-named him GARBO; the Germans called him ARABEL. With his case officer Tomás Harris, he built a network of imaginary agents that grew to [[27]], all paid by Germany.

**File: Operation Mincemeat · 1943** (Naval Intelligence)
To hide the real target, Sicily, British officers planted fake invasion plans on a dead body dressed as "Major William Martin" of the Royal Marines. The submarine HMS Seraph released the body off Huelva, Spain, on [[30 April 1943]], and a Spanish fisherman found it. The letters pointed Germany toward [[Greece and Sardinia]]. Decoded German messages later showed Germany sending reinforcements there.

**File: Garbo's D-Day message · 1944** (Security Service)
On the night of D-Day, MI5 let Garbo radio a warning that the invasion had begun, [[timed to arrive too late to help]] Germany. It made the Germans trust him even more. Three days later he told them Normandy was [[only a diversion]], helping keep German troops waiting at Calais.

### Drawer 6: ULTRA LIAISON

**File: Special Liaison Units · 1940** (Special Liaison Units)
Special Liaison Units, organized by Group Captain [[F. W. Winterbotham]], carried Ultra to commanders in the field. An officer showed the message, waited while it was read, then [[took the paper back]]. Only a handful of senior officers knew Ultra came from codebreaking, and they were forbidden to reveal it.

**File: Rommel strikes back · 1942** (Eighth Army)
On [[21 January 1942]], Rommel counterattacked in North Africa and caught the British by surprise. His forces retook [[Benghazi]] on 29 January, and by early February the British had fallen back to the [[Gazala]] line. Ultra had shown fresh tanks arriving at Tripoli, but not Rommel's plan to attack.

**File: Convoys to Africa · 1942** (Malta)
Rommel's army depended on supply ships crossing the Mediterranean from Italy. Ultra often revealed when and where the convoys would sail. Before planes and submarines from [[Malta]] attacked, the British usually sent a [[reconnaissance plane]] first, so each strike seemed to follow a lucky sighting instead of a broken code.

## Part 2: Intel reports on cards

Each role's card shows three reports. A **loyal agent's** card shows all three true versions. A **double agent** picks one report to replace with its fake. In the interrogation's "prove it" step, the three choices are always: the true detail, the fake, and the third choice, shown in random order. Every report can be checked against an Archive file.

**Notes for review:**
- **Believable on purpose.** Some fakes are real things from elsewhere in the war: U-110 is the other famous captured U-boat, Tobruk fell later in 1942, and Sicily was the real target. A fake should tempt students who half-remember. The truth is always in the Archive.
- **Free to change.** You can swap any fake or third choice that feels too easy or too hard.
