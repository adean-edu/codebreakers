The computer nobody knew about

In 1944, engineer Tommy Flowers and his team built Colossus. It is often called the world’s first programmable electronic computer.

  • Its job: break “Tunny,” the cipher of Hitler’s high command.
  • By 1945: ten machines, working day and night.
  • After the war: most were broken up, and the secret was kept until the 1970s.

Dorothy Du Boisson and Elsie Booker operating Colossus. Photo: UK National Archives, public domain.

Here's a surprise: one of the first computers was built to break codes, and almost nobody knew. In this photo, two members of the Women's Royal Naval Service, Dorothy Du Boisson and Elsie Booker, are operating Colossus. Engineer Tommy Flowers and his team built it in 1944 to help read Tunny, the cipher used by Hitler's high command, which was even harder than Enigma. By the end of the war there were ten Colossus machines. Afterwards most were broken up and the secret was kept until the 1970s, so for years other computers got the credit for being first.
