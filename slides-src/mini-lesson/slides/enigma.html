A machine with too many settings

159,000,000,000,000,000,000

That’s about how many ways an army Enigma could be set up, and the settings changed every day.

  • 1932: Polish mathematician Marian Rejewski breaks it using math.
  • 1939: Poland shares its methods with Britain and France.
  • 1940 onward: Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman’s “bombe” machines test settings at speed.
This is a real Enigma machine, now in the US National Cryptologic Museum. You typed a letter on the keyboard, and a different letter lit up on the lampboard. It could be set up in about 159 million million million different ways, and the settings changed every day at midnight. The Germans were sure nobody could read it. But in 1932 a young Polish mathematician, Marian Rejewski, broke it using mathematics. In July 1939, weeks before the war, Poland shared its methods and replica machines with Britain and France. At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman designed machines called bombes that could test settings far faster than people could.
