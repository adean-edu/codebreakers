More roles in the secret war

The photo detectives

An RAF spy-plane photo of Peenemünde, 23 June 1943. A: a V-2 rocket on its trailer. B: a mobile test stand. C: the assembly hall. Photo: RAF, public domain.

  • Unarmed spy planes flew over enemy territory and took millions of photographs. Interpreters at RAF Medmenham, west of London, studied them.
  • With a stereoscope, two photos side by side popped into 3D, so they could measure buildings and weapons.
  • In 1943, interpreter Constance Babington Smith spotted a tiny flying bomb on a launch ramp at Peenemünde, Germany’s secret weapons base.
  • Photos also helped plan D-Day, checking beaches, defenses and bridges.

In the game, Resistance Couriers smuggle out proof about the same rocket program.

Not all intelligence came from radio messages. RAF reconnaissance planes, stripped of guns so they could fly higher and faster, photographed enemy territory, and the photos went to the Central Interpretation Unit at RAF Medmenham, a country house west of London. This is one of those real photos: Peenemünde on 23 June 1943. Point out label A: that small shape is a V-2 rocket lying on its trailer. Interpreters, many of them women, studied pictures like this under magnifiers. Using a stereoscope, two overlapping photos could be seen in 3D, so they could measure the size of buildings and weapons. Later in 1943, interpreter Constance Babington Smith spotted a tiny aircraft-shaped object on a launch ramp at Peenemünde: one of Germany's secret flying bombs. Photo interpreters also helped plan D-Day. In the game, the Resistance Courier role tracks the same secret rocket program from the ground.
